An air-gapped Bitcoin signer and offline key manager. Rust runs directly on a Linux kernel, with networking and internal storage disabled at the kernel level. It runs on PC and Raspberry Pi, and on Mac soon.

Keys from dice

Your entropy verifiably turned into words

Roll every bit yourself and watch the words appear under a binary verification layer that anyone can follow along with. Full word lists included for further manual verification.

Runs from memory, keeps nothing

Pull the stick, and nothing is attached

Faraday loads into memory and runs from there. Its kernel has no network stack and no driver for the computer's own disks, so with the boot stick out it has no network and no drive of any kind.

No stick is read while a vault is unlocked or a key loaded. Vaults and keys load only with no stick attached.

Use it statelessly: nothing is written to the computer, locking wipes every key and starts the app afresh, and powering off loses everything in memory. Only what you write to a stick is kept.

No desktop

You're not looking at a desktop

There is no X11, no Wayland, no window manager, no GPU driver, no toolkit and no browser engine. The firmware sets up a framebuffer, the kernel hands it over, and Faraday, one Rust program, lays out every screen, rasterises its own fonts and writes every pixel itself. The blinking current isn't really a blinking cursor.

Keys, clicks and touches arrive as raw kernel input events mapped to a location on the screen, not a button or a field. Less software between you and your keys is less to attack.

Vaults

One password to your dashboard

A vault is one optional file on the stick, loaded into RAM at boot and never unlocked until the stick is pulled. Vaults are sealed with Argon2id and XChaCha20-Poly1305. Roll dice to generate strong EFF wordlist passwords. Verify the password creation yourself with on screen numbering and the included word lists. Unlock it and Faraday loads its keys and wallets, the whole of it on a laptop's screen.

Backups and inheritance

Backup maps so you see the full picture

Faraday's backup plan says what goes where, and checks each seed copied by hand. The map shows an overview of all the pieces. Faraday's Spend tab walks first time spenders from the envelope to a sent transaction. Faraday also signs for miniscript wallets with an inheritance path.

Supply chain

A generic computer or Pi, so nothing to target

A hardware wallet is made for one job, so it is worth tampering with: in the factory, in the post or in the shop, an attacker knows what it will hold. Buying one also puts your name and address on a maker's customer list, and such lists have leaked.

Faraday runs on an ordinary laptop, one of millions, bought anywhere or already yours. Nothing bought says you hold keys.

Faraday fits naturally into a multi-vendor multisig setup, utilizing a device you likely already own.

Tools for advanced users

Every flow on one page, with its standards

Beyond seeds and wallets: SLIP-39 shares, Seed XOR, codex32, BIP-85 child seeds and passwords, MuSig2, FROST, Taproot multisig, miniscript, silent payments, GPG and Secure Boot keys, and passwords with TOTP in a vault.

Faraday Starter

A helper to get started

PCs can only start Faraday's stick with Secure Boot off, and each maker puts that setting in a different firmware menu.

Faraday Starter is an app for Windows and Linux, with macOS coming soon. It writes Faraday to your stick, then shows the steps for rebooting into Faraday. Secure Boot is a BIOS setting, which gets turned off temporarily to boot Faraday, and back on to boot your normal OS. Faraday Starter walks you through it.

      Questions

      Is Faraday a wallet?

      It is a keystore and a signer. It holds keys and it signs. A watch-only wallet on another computer shows balances, builds transactions and sends them; the two pass PSBTs by QR code or on a stick. It is recommended to not store all private signing information (keys, passphrases) inside of Faraday. Keys should be stored in different physical locations.

      What does it keep when it powers off?

      Nothing: it is stateless. The whole system loads into memory from one file. Nothing is written to any internal storage device. The only thing that persists is what you choose to take out, either by handwriting or in a vault on a USB stick. This is the essence of Faraday.

      What is an offline computer, and why not use one?

      An offline computer, sometimes referred to as an airgapped computer, runs a full operating system, including a desktop and often a web browser with an HTML wallet tool. It can work, but the attack surface is huge compared to Faraday: the operating system, the browser engine, and every library and script the tool loads are each a separate trust root.

      Web browsers are one of the most common and critical initial vectors used in sophisticated exploit chains that ultimately target the kernel. With network drivers and internal storage baked into the system, such "offline" computers remain a juicy attack surface for attackers. Faraday is one program on a kernel built without a network stack, internal storage, a desktop or a browser.

      How is it different from other stateless signers?

      Stateless signers on small boards keep nothing between sessions, as Faraday does, and are made for people who already know the flows. Faraday keeps that model and is made for everyone: Guided mode writes out each step, and the Spend tab walks someone spending an inherited wallet for the first time from the envelope to a sent transaction. Vaults are an additional benefit of Faraday, as paper backups can easily expose private data. Each user has a different need, and careful thought is required to ensure a robust, but secure backup plan. Faraday is an additional tool in your toolkit to this end.

      Does it touch the laptop's own disk?

      No. Its kernel has no driver for SATA, NVMe or eMMC disks, so the computer's own disk cannot be read, written or mounted. Nothing can protect against compromised hardware, but with literally billions of computers in the world, it is not difficult to find an untampered device to use with Faraday.

      Can it go online by accident?

      Its kernel is built with no network stack: no Ethernet, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or USB network driver, and no sockets. If you use Faraday Starter, the app will check for any risky external peripherals (smart TVs are the main concern here) and give you a warning.

      Why a laptop and not a small device?

      A laptop's screen shows significantly more information at once. Key signing needs to be done with careful thought, and seeing the full picture at a glance can help avoid mistakes. Laptops are preferred because they can be used in privacy, and its screen, keyboard and webcam are inside one case (no external peripherals required).

      Does it run on a Mac?

      Not yet: Mac support is coming soon. Intel Macs with 64-bit EFI will start the same stick as a PC, and M1 and M2 Macs a stick image of their own, set up once through Faraday Starter. M3 follows once Asahi Linux supports it, and M4 and M5 once Asahi's boot code starts on them; this follows Apple's and Asahi Linux's documentation. The download page has more.

      What is a vault?

      Faraday vaults use a custom format, but standard cryptography. Any AI can analyze a Faraday vault and understand its structure. The thing that keeps it safe is a strong passphrase over good cryptography. Vaults are sealed with Argon2id and XChaCha20-Poly1305. Its passphrase can be rolled with dice on the EFF word lists within Faraday.

      Is the demo the real app?

      Yes: the same app, compiled to WebAssembly. It takes only eleven public seeds, and every address it shows has a broken checksum, so no wallet will send to one.

      Is it ready for real money?

      Faraday 0.1.4 is a pre-release. Only time and community acceptance will prove its worth. Point your clanker or your eyes at it and scrutinize it to the max!

      How did Faraday come to be, and who is making it?

      I, @bitcoinpasada, am the project creator. Opensigner, the cryptographic signing code underlying Faraday, was developed by an experienced software developer as a personal project. After seeing that project, and in the wake of numerous hardware wallet exploits, I realized the potential for a new signer and keystore to meet the needs of the Bitcoin community. I hope you'll join me in helping to test and develop the project.
